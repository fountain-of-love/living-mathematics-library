# How Rotation Becomes a Wave

## Core Distinction

Rotational mathematics separates three layers:

1. the underlying operation,
2. the trajectory generated by repeated or continuous operation,
3. the values observed when that trajectory is sampled through a projection.

For rotation, \(i\), \(\pi\), sine, and cosine are not unrelated objects. They are different views of the same circular structure.

## Rotating Point on the Unit Circle

Let

$$
z(t)=e^{it}.
$$

This describes a point rotating on the unit circle. By Euler's formula,

$$
e^{it}=\cos t+i\sin t.
$$

The roles separate as follows:

| Layer | Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Primitive operation | multiplication by \(i\) | quarter-turn rotation |
| Continuous generator | \(e^{it}\) | rotation evolving with \(t\) |
| Natural scale | \(\pi\) | angular measure determined by circular closure |
| Full period | \(2\pi\) | return to the starting state |
| Horizontal observation | \(x(t)=\cos t\) | projection onto the horizontal axis |
| Vertical observation | \(y(t)=\sin t\) | projection onto the vertical axis |
| Special events | \(0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2},2\pi\) | symmetry, zero, and turning points |

## Projection Creates Oscillation

Observe only the horizontal coordinate:

$$
x(t)=\cos t.
$$

At key quarter-turns,

$$
\cos 0=1,\qquad
\cos\frac{\pi}{2}=0,\qquad
\cos\pi=-1,\qquad
\cos\frac{3\pi}{2}=0,\qquad
\cos 2\pi=1.
$$

The observed sequence is

$$
1\rightarrow 0\rightarrow -1\rightarrow 0\rightarrow 1.
$$

The point itself is not moving back and forth. It is continuously rotating. The back-and-forth motion is produced by observing only one coordinate.

Thus

$$
\text{rotation}\xrightarrow{\text{projection}}\text{oscillation}.
$$

The circle and the wave represent the same dynamics viewed through different lenses.

## The Relation Between \(i\) and \(\pi\)

Multiplication by \(i\) performs a quarter turn:

$$
1\rightarrow i\rightarrow -1\rightarrow -i\rightarrow 1.
$$

One quarter turn corresponds to angle \(\pi/2\). Therefore

$$
i=e^{i\pi/2}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
i^2=e^{i\pi}=-1,
$$

$$
i^3=e^{i3\pi/2}=-i,
$$

$$
i^4=e^{i2\pi}=1.
$$

The distinction is:

$$
i=\text{rotational transformation},
$$

whereas

$$
\pi=\text{natural angular scale of rotation}.
$$

They are connected by the translation

$$
i=e^{i\pi/2}.
$$

## Translation Table

| Source Structure | Transformation | Observation | Emergent Relation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rotation | \(e^{it}\) | horizontal projection | \(\cos t\) |
| Rotation | \(e^{it}\) | vertical projection | \(\sin t\) |
| Quarter turn | \(i\) | angular representation | \(i=e^{i\pi/2}\) |
| Half turn | \(i^2\) | angular representation | \(-1=e^{i\pi}\) |
| Full turn | \(i^4\) | angular representation | \(1=e^{i2\pi}\) |
| Circular motion | projection | zeros | \(t=\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\) |
| Circular motion | full recurrence | period | \(2\pi\) |

## General Grammar

The structural grammar is:

$$
\text{Structure}
\rightarrow
\text{Dynamics}
\rightarrow
\text{Observer}
\rightarrow
\text{Observable}
\rightarrow
\text{Invariant or event}.
$$

For the unit circle:

$$
\text{circle}
\rightarrow
e^{it}
\rightarrow
\Re
\rightarrow
\cos t
\rightarrow
\cos t=0\text{ at }\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi.
$$

## Course Note

An observed zero is not necessarily a zero of the underlying system. The rotating point satisfies

$$
|e^{it}|=1
$$

for every \(t\). Yet its horizontal projection may vanish:

$$
\Re(e^{it})=0.
$$

Thus

$$
\text{zero of an observation}\neq\text{zero of the underlying system}.
$$

An observed zero is a geometric alignment revealed by a chosen observer. This distinction is essential when constructing a mathematical ontology of structures, operations, observations, and events.
